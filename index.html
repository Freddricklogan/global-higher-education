Re Global Higher Education & Internationalization

Internationalization is a purpose-driven institutional process — not a study-abroad office — and leading it well means balancing strategy, mobility, quality, and equity across borders.

  • Internationalization integrates an international, intercultural, and global dimension into teaching, research, and service — a deliberate institutional response to globalization, not a synonym for it.
  • Institutions go global for academic, economic, political, and social/cultural rationales, each carrying trade-offs such as commercialization, brain drain, and inequity of access.
  • Comprehensive strategy is assembled from distinct levers — student mobility, branch campuses, partnerships, internationalized curriculum, research collaboration, and virtual exchange / COIL.
  • Global systems differ in degree structure, funding, and quality assurance; the Bologna Process and ECTS make European credentials comparable and portable across 40+ countries.
  • Global rankings reward research and English-language output over teaching and equity, so leaders treat them as one contested signal — useful for benchmarking, dangerous as strategy.
  • Equity and decolonization demand reciprocal partnerships and internationalization at home, so global learning reaches the majority of students who never travel.
Orientation

What internationalization actually means

Internationalization is not a single program or a study-abroad office. Jane Knight's widely adopted definition frames it as a process woven through the core of the institution.

Knight's definition

A process, not a product

Internationalization is "the process of integrating an international, intercultural, or global dimension into the purpose, functions, or delivery of post-secondary education" — that is, into teaching, research, and service. It is a means to institutional goals, not an end in itself.

Key nuance: Knight distinguishes internationalization from globalization — globalization is the flow of ideas, people, and capital across borders (a context); internationalization is how a nation or institution deliberately responds to it.

Comprehensive internationalization (John Hudzik / ACE) is the commitment to infuse international and comparative perspectives throughout the entire institution — mission, leadership, curriculum, faculty, and partnerships — rather than treating it as a set of isolated activities. "Internationalization at home" (Beelen & Jones) ensures the 90%+ of students who never travel still gain intercultural and global competence.
Drivers & dangers

Rationales for going global — and the risks

Knight and de Wit group the motivations for internationalization into four families. Naming your dominant rationale clarifies strategy — and exposes the trade-offs each one carries.

Academic

Quality & reputation

Enhancing academic standards, research capacity, curriculum, and institutional profile through global engagement and benchmarking.

Economic

Revenue & competitiveness

Tuition income from international students, workforce and skills development, and national economic competitiveness.

Political

Diplomacy & influence

Foreign policy, soft power, national security, and strategic ties — education as an instrument of international relations.

Social / cultural

Understanding & citizenship

Intercultural understanding, global citizenship, national cultural identity, and preparing graduates for a connected world.

Internationalization strategy explorer

Comprehensive internationalization is assembled from distinct levers. Select a lever to see its definition, the benefits it delivers, and the challenges leaders must manage. No single lever is a strategy on its own.

Student & scholar mobility

The physical movement of students, faculty, and staff across borders for study, teaching, or research — the most visible and traditional lever, spanning short-term study abroad, credit mobility, and full degree mobility.

Definition: inbound (hosting international students) and outbound (sending home students abroad) flows, credit vs. degree mobility.

  • BenefitsIntercultural competence, language gains, employability, campus diversity, tuition revenue
  • ChallengesCost & equity of access, health/safety & risk, credit transfer, visa dependence, re-entry support
  • ReachesA minority of students — hence "at home" complements it
  • Owned byStudy abroad & international student offices

International branch campuses

An institution establishes a physical presence in another country, awarding its own credential in the host location — a form of program and provider mobility that moves the institution to the student.

Definition: cross-border delivery where the awarding institution operates a campus abroad (e.g. education hubs in the Gulf, Southeast Asia, China).

  • BenefitsAccess for non-mobile students, global brand, new markets, host-country capacity building
  • ChallengesQuality assurance across jurisdictions, academic freedom, financial risk, cultural & legal fit
  • ModelWholly owned, joint venture, or government-partnered
  • GovernanceHome & host accreditation both apply

Partnerships & joint / dual degrees

Formal agreements between institutions in different countries — from student exchange and articulation to joint degrees (one shared credential) and dual/double degrees (two credentials) delivered collaboratively.

Definition: collaborative programs, consortia, and Memoranda of Understanding that pool curricula, faculty, and recognition.

  • BenefitsShared expertise, recruitment pipelines, cost-sharing, mutual recognition of credit
  • ChallengesCredit & degree recognition, quality alignment, sustainability, unequal partner power
  • SpectrumMOU → exchange → articulation → joint/dual degree
  • Success factorReciprocity & clear governance, not one-way flows

Internationalized curriculum

Embedding international, intercultural, and global perspectives into the content, learning outcomes, and teaching of programs — the heart of "internationalization at home," reaching every student regardless of mobility.

Definition: Internationalization of the Curriculum (Betty Leask) — intentional design so all graduates develop global and intercultural capabilities.

  • BenefitsEquitable global learning, employability, inclusive of non-mobile & local students
  • ChallengesFaculty buy-in & development, avoiding tokenism, assessing intercultural outcomes
  • ScopeContent, pedagogy, languages, and hidden curriculum
  • Owned byFaculty & academic units, not just the IO

International research collaboration

Cross-border co-authorship, joint grants, shared infrastructure, and researcher mobility that address problems no single nation can solve alone — increasingly the engine of global knowledge production.

Definition: collaborative research networks, international co-publication, and mobility of scholars and doctoral candidates.

  • BenefitsHigher impact & citation, access to funding & facilities, capacity building, global challenges
  • ChallengesEquitable authorship, research security & ethics, IP, "helicopter/parachute" research
  • TrendRising share of internationally co-authored papers
  • EthicsFair credit & benefit-sharing with Global South partners

Virtual exchange / COIL

Collaborative Online International Learning connects classes in different countries through technology-enabled, sustained collaboration — delivering intercultural learning without the cost or carbon of travel.

Definition: COIL (SUNY origin) & virtual exchange — co-taught, project-based online collaboration between geographically separated students.

  • BenefitsLow-cost, inclusive, scalable; reaches students who cannot travel; sustainable
  • ChallengesTime zones, digital access & equity, faculty design effort, depth vs. in-person immersion
  • GrowthAccelerated sharply after 2020
  • Best asA complement to — not replacement for — physical mobility
Student mobility

The flows of students across borders

Mobility is measured in two directions and two durations. Leaders design programs that maximize learning while managing cost, safety, and access.

Directions & durations

  • Inbound — international students hosted on the home campus
  • Outbound — home students who study abroad
  • Degree mobility — enrolling abroad for a full credential
  • Credit mobility — a term/year abroad counting toward the home degree (e.g. exchange, Erasmus+)
  • Short-term — faculty-led programs, summer, and field study, now the largest share of U.S. outbound

Global flows (general patterns, not precise stats)

  • Leading host countries — the United States, United Kingdom, Australia, Canada, Germany, and France attract large shares of internationally mobile students
  • Leading sending countries — China and India are the largest sources of internationally mobile students, followed by others across Asia and Africa
  • Emerging hubs — the Gulf states, China, and Malaysia host branch campuses and regional students
  • Flows shift with visa policy, currency, geopolitics, and post-study work rights

Leading a study-abroad program — the administrator's responsibilities

  • Program planning — learning outcomes, academic integration, credit approval, provider vetting
  • Health, safety & security — risk assessment, insurance, emergency & evacuation protocols, 24/7 contact
  • Risk management — travel warnings, duty of care, incident response, crisis communication
  • Equity & access — scholarships, credit-bearing options, advising to broaden who participates
  • Pre-departure & re-entry — orientation, cultural preparation, and support returning home
  • Compliance — Title IX abroad, Clery Act awareness, accessibility, and student conduct
Equity in education abroad is a persistent challenge: participants have historically skewed toward higher-income, majority-race, non-STEM, able-bodied students. Inclusive program design — funding, short-term and credit-bearing options, and targeted advising — is now a core leadership priority (see NAFSA and the Institute of International Education's efforts).
International student services

Supporting international students across the journey

Recruiting and enrolling international students is only the beginning. A comprehensive support system spans the full lifecycle from inquiry to alumni.

Recruitment & marketing

Reaching prospective students through agents, fairs, digital outreach, and partnerships — balancing enrollment goals with ethical recruitment and honest representation.

Admissions & credential evaluation

Assessing foreign transcripts and qualifications for equivalency (via evaluators such as NACES-member services or WES), plus English proficiency (TOEFL/IELTS) and holistic review.

Visas & immigration compliance

Issuing the Form I-20 (F-1) or DS-2019 (J-1), maintaining SEVIS records, and advising on status — the Designated School Official (DSO) role. Compliance protects the institution's ability to enroll international students.

Orientation & arrival

Immigration check-in, cultural adjustment, academic expectations, banking, housing, and health — front-loading the support that prevents later crises.

Ongoing support & belonging

Advising, counseling, mentoring, English support, and community — addressing culture shock, isolation, and academic-system differences.

Work authorization & transition

Advising on on-campus work, CPT and OPT (practical training), and post-study pathways — a decisive factor in destination choice.

SEVIS (the Student and Exchange Visitor Information System) is the U.S. federal database that tracks F and J visa holders. The DSO and Responsible Officer report enrollment, address, and status changes — making immigration advising a regulated, high-stakes function, not a clerical one. NAFSA: Association of International Educators is the leading professional home for this work.
Comparative systems & quality

How global higher education is structured and assured

Working across borders requires reading other systems — their degree structures, quality regimes, and the rankings that increasingly shape institutional behavior.

Bologna Process / EHEA

Convergence in Europe

Launched by the 1999 Bologna Declaration, the Bologna Process built the European Higher Education Area — harmonizing degree structures into a three-cycle system (bachelor's / master's / doctorate), the ECTS credit system, quality assurance standards, and the Diploma Supplement to make degrees comparable and portable across 40+ countries.

Degree structures

Systems differ in shape

  • Length and structure of the bachelor's degree vary (3 vs. 4 years)
  • Grading scales and credit systems are not equivalent (ECTS ≠ U.S. credit hours)
  • Binary systems separate research universities from applied/vocational institutions
  • Governance ranges from state-controlled to market-driven
Global rankings

Powerful — and contested

The major rankings — QS, Times Higher Education (THE), and the Academic Ranking of World Universities (ARWU / Shanghai) — shape reputation, recruitment, and policy.

Critiques: over-weight research over teaching; favor large, wealthy, English-language, science-heavy institutions; rely on reputation surveys and gameable metrics; narrow the definition of "quality"; and can distort institutional priorities.

Quality assurance

Trust across borders

Accreditation and quality assurance vary by country — from U.S. regional/programmatic accreditors to national QA agencies abroad. Cross-border collaboration relies on mutual recognition.

Key instruments: the INQAAHE network, the European ESG standards and ENQA, and UNESCO's Global Convention on the recognition of qualifications — all working to combat diploma mills and enable credential portability.

Comparative systems

Comparative higher education systems

Working across borders means reading systems that are structured on different logics. Descriptions are structural generalizations, not precise statistics.

Comparative literacy (Altbach) is a prerequisite for equitable, workable cross-border collaboration.
Deep dive

The Bologna Process, in depth

Begun with the 1999 Bologna Declaration, the Bologna Process is a voluntary intergovernmental reform that built the European Higher Education Area (EHEA), now spanning more than 40 countries. Its aim is comparable, compatible, and coherent degrees that let students and graduates move freely.

Three-cycle system

Bachelor · Master · Doctorate

Bologna restructured degrees into three sequential cycles. The first cycle (bachelor's) gives access to the second cycle (master's), which in turn gives access to the third cycle (doctorate). A shared reference framework of qualifications describes each cycle by learning outcomes, so a "master's" means broadly the same level of competence across the EHEA.

ECTS credits

Currency of learning

The European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System expresses study in credits tied to student workload and learning outcomes — a full academic year is conventionally 60 ECTS. ECTS lets credit earned at one institution transfer and accumulate at another, making credit mobility (and Erasmus+ exchange) administratively workable.

Diploma Supplement

Making a degree legible abroad

The Diploma Supplement is a standardized document, issued with the degree, that describes the nature, level, context, content, and status of the studies completed. It travels with the qualification so employers and institutions in other countries can understand what a credential actually represents.

Mobility & recognition

Movement by design

Bologna set explicit mobility goals — a benchmark aspiration that a meaningful share of graduates should have a study or training period abroad — supported by fair recognition of qualifications and credit (the Lisbon Recognition Convention) so that time abroad counts rather than being lost.

Quality assurance

The ESG, ENQA, and mutual trust

Comparability only works if quality is trusted across borders. Bologna therefore built a shared quality-assurance architecture: the Standards and Guidelines for Quality Assurance in the EHEA (ESG) define common expectations for internal QA, external review, and QA agencies themselves. The European Association for Quality Assurance in Higher Education (ENQA) is the membership body for QA agencies that meet those standards, and the European Quality Assurance Register (EQAR) lists agencies operating in line with the ESG — the concept being that if agencies are trustworthy, the degrees they assure can be trusted across the whole area.

The through-line: three cycles make degrees comparable, ECTS makes learning transferable, the Diploma Supplement makes credentials legible, recognition conventions make mobility count, and the ESG/ENQA framework makes quality trustworthy. Together they turn 40+ national systems into one mobility space.

Global rankings — what the metrics actually reward

Three ranking systems dominate global attention. Each claims to measure "the world's best universities," but each is really a weighted formula — and what a formula weights is what institutions are pushed to chase. Select a system to see, conceptually, what it rewards and where it is critiqued.

Academic Ranking of World Universities (ARWU / Shanghai)

First published in 2003 by Shanghai Jiao Tong University, ARWU is the most research-and-elite-prize oriented of the major rankings. It leans on objective bibliometric and award-based indicators rather than opinion surveys.

Conceptually rewards: Nobel Prizes and Fields Medals (among alumni and staff), highly cited researchers, and papers in the most selective science journals.

  • RewardsElite research output, prizes, citation superstars
  • Data sourceBibliometric and award databases (no reputation survey)
  • Tilts towardLarge, old, science-and-medicine-heavy universities
  • Blind spotTeaching quality, arts & humanities, smaller institutions

Times Higher Education (THE) World University Rankings

THE uses a broad basket of indicators grouped around teaching, research environment, research quality (including citation impact), international outlook, and industry income — blending objective data with large reputation surveys.

Conceptually rewards: citation impact, research income and productivity, reputation among academics, and international staff, students, and co-authorship.

  • RewardsCitations, research environment, reputation, international mix
  • Data sourceInstitutional data + a global reputation survey
  • Tilts towardResearch-intensive, well-funded, English-language institutions
  • Blind spotReputation surveys reproduce existing prestige; teaching proxied indirectly

QS World University Rankings

QS places heavy weight on reputation surveys — a large academic reputation survey plus an employer reputation survey — alongside faculty–student ratio, citations per faculty, and internationalization measures.

Conceptually rewards: academic and employer opinion, a favorable staffing ratio, citation counts, and international faculty and student presence.

  • RewardsPerceived reputation, employability signal, international mix
  • Data sourceVery large academic + employer surveys, plus metrics
  • Tilts towardGlobally visible brand-name institutions
  • Blind spotSurvey-driven scores are the most susceptible to reputation lag and gaming

Bias toward research & English

  • Formulas privilege research volume and citations over teaching and learning
  • English-language publication and indexing advantage Anglophone systems
  • Science & medicine count more than arts, humanities, and professional fields

Gaming & distortion

  • Reputation surveys can be lobbied; metrics can be optimized rather than improved
  • Documented manipulation (mis-reported data, citation and affiliation gaming) has forced withdrawals and corrections
  • Institutions may reallocate resources to move the ranking, not the mission

What rankings miss

  • Equity, access, and social mobility of graduates
  • Regional service, community engagement, and public good
  • Student experience and actual learning gains
The leadership takeaway (after Marginson): rankings are a form of global positional competition that can concentrate prestige and resources among already-dominant universities. Read them as one contested signal among many — useful for benchmarking, dangerous as a strategy. Alternative frameworks (e.g. rankings oriented to impact and the Sustainable Development Goals) try to reward a wider definition of value.

Transnational education (TNE)

Transnational (or cross-border) education is provision in which the learners are located in a different country from the awarding institution. Rather than moving the student to the provider, TNE moves the programme or the provider to the student. Select a model to weigh its trade-offs.

Choose a TNE model

Branch campus
Franchising / validation
Joint & double degrees
Distance / online
Branch campus

Provider moves abroad

The awarding institution runs a physical campus in a host country and grants its own credential there.

  • Pro: home brand & credential, access for non-mobile students, host capacity building
  • Con: high capital & financial risk, dual-jurisdiction quality assurance, academic-freedom tensions
Franchising

Programme moves abroad

A local partner delivers the awarding institution's programme under licence (franchise) or has its own programme approved (validation).

  • Pro: low capital, fast reach, uses local partner's footprint
  • Con: quality drift & oversight risk, brand exposure, reputational dependence on the partner
Joint / double

Institutions collaborate

Partners co-design a programme awarding a single shared credential (joint) or two credentials (double / dual).

  • Pro: shared expertise, mutual recognition, recruitment pipelines, deep partnership
  • Con: complex governance & recognition, quality alignment, sustainability if a champion leaves
Distance / online

Programme travels digitally

The credential is delivered wholly or largely online across borders, sometimes with local support centres.

  • Pro: scalable, low marginal cost, flexible for working & non-mobile learners
  • Con: digital-access equity, engagement & completion, credential recognition, weaker cultural immersion
Regulatory reality: TNE typically sits under two regulatory regimes at once — the awarding country's and the host country's — and quality can vary from excellent to exploitative. Frameworks such as the UNESCO/OECD guidelines on quality provision in cross-border higher education exist precisely to protect students from low-quality or fraudulent provision.
Equity-minded internationalization

Virtual exchange & COIL, in depth

Collaborative Online International Learning (COIL) and the broader family of virtual exchange connect students in different countries through sustained, technology-enabled collaboration built into the curriculum. Pioneered at the SUNY COIL Center, it is increasingly framed as an equity strategy — global learning for the many who cannot travel.

What makes it COIL (not just a video call)

  • Co-taught & co-designed by faculty at two or more institutions in different countries
  • Curriculum-embedded — part of a for-credit course, not an add-on
  • Sustained collaboration — students work together over weeks on shared, project-based tasks
  • Intercultural by design — reflection on cultural difference is an explicit learning outcome
  • Blends synchronous and asynchronous work to bridge time zones

Why it is equity-minded

  • Removes the cost, visa, and time barriers that exclude most students from mobility
  • Reaches first-generation, low-income, caregiving, disabled, and working students
  • Builds reciprocal partnerships where both sides' students gain, not one-way flows
  • Lower carbon footprint than physical mobility
  • Scales intercultural learning across a whole cohort, not a self-selected few
Positioning: the strongest programs treat virtual exchange as a complement to physical mobility and internationalization at home — a way to democratize global learning — rather than a cheaper substitute for study abroad. The digital divide is itself an equity risk, so access to devices, connectivity, and support has to be designed in.
Practice

The partnerships lifecycle

International partnerships fail more often from weak process than weak intentions. A disciplined lifecycle — from scoping to a deliberate sunset — keeps agreements strategic, reciprocal, and sustainable rather than accumulating dormant MOUs.

Scoping & strategic fit

Test the idea against institutional mission and priorities before investing. Who initiated it and why? Is there genuine academic complementarity and reciprocity — or is one side simply seeking revenue or prestige? Define the intended value for students, faculty, and both institutions.

Due diligence

Vet the prospective partner: accreditation and quality-assurance status, financial stability, academic reputation, legal and regulatory environment, academic-freedom and human-rights considerations, and any reputational or research-security risks. This is where problems are cheapest to avoid.

MOU & formal agreement

Move from a non-binding Memorandum of Understanding (a statement of intent) to a specific, binding agreement that names deliverables, responsibilities, finances, intellectual property, data protection, dispute resolution, duration, and exit terms. Involve legal counsel and academic governance early.

Implementation & stewardship

Assign a named owner on each side, resource the work, and communicate. Most partnerships live or die on relationships and follow-through — build in regular contact, shared expectations, and cultural sensitivity rather than signing and walking away.

Review, renewal & sunset

Evaluate against the outcomes defined at scoping: activity, quality, reciprocity, and value. Then decide deliberately — deepen, renew, or gracefully end. Retiring a partnership that no longer serves either institution is a success of governance, not a failure; it frees capacity for ones that do.

The MOU trap: many institutions accumulate dozens of signed MOUs that never produce activity. Treat the signature as the start of work, not the achievement — and audit the portfolio periodically so that dormant agreements are closed rather than counted.
Enablers

Funding & mobility programs

Flagship public programs finance much of the world's academic mobility and exchange, often with explicit diplomatic or regional-integration goals. Descriptions below are conceptual — no invented figures.

United States

Fulbright Program

The flagship U.S. international exchange program, established in 1946 under legislation sponsored by Senator J. William Fulbright and administered by the U.S. Department of State. It funds students, scholars, and teachers to study, research, and teach across many partner countries in both directions.

Rationale: mutual understanding and educational diplomacy — a classic instrument of soft power.

European Union

Erasmus+

The EU's flagship program for education, training, youth, and sport. Building on the original Erasmus student-exchange scheme, Erasmus+ funds credit mobility, joint programs (including Erasmus Mundus joint master's), staff exchange, and cooperation partnerships across Europe and, increasingly, with partner regions worldwide.

Rationale: European integration, employability, and a shared higher-education area.

National scholarships

Government scholarship schemes

Many governments run national scholarship and mobility schemes to attract talent, build capacity, or project influence — for example the UK's Chevening and Commonwealth scholarships, Germany's DAAD support, and the Chinese Government Scholarship. Some fund inbound international students; others send home nationals abroad.

Rationale: talent attraction, development, and diplomatic ties.

Reading the map: mobility funding is rarely neutral charity — it advances the funder's goals, whether educational diplomacy (Fulbright), regional integration (Erasmus+), capacity building, or talent competition. Understanding the why behind a program helps leaders align institutional participation with genuine mutual benefit.
Ethics

Decolonizing internationalization

A critical strand of scholarship argues that internationalization, left unexamined, can reproduce colonial patterns — flows of prestige, knowledge, and money that run from a dominant "center" to a dependent "periphery" (Altbach). Decolonizing it means confronting those power asymmetries directly.

Naming the power asymmetries

  • Prestige, rankings, and citation systems privilege the Global North and English
  • Curricula and "world-class" models often default to Western canons and epistemologies
  • Partnerships can be structured so one side sets terms and the other supplies data or students
  • Recruitment can extract tuition and talent from lower-income systems

Sustainable & reciprocal partnerships

  • Co-create research questions, agendas, and authorship rather than extracting
  • Share funding, infrastructure, credit, and capacity — not just risk
  • Reject "helicopter" / "parachute" research that takes without benefit-sharing
  • Center local knowledge systems and epistemic diversity (Stein; Leask)

Brain drain vs. brain circulation

  • Brain drain: talent leaves sending countries and does not return, depleting home capacity
  • Brain circulation: mobile scholars move, return, and connect — building diaspora networks and two-way benefit
  • Policy and partnership design can tilt the balance from drain toward circulation
  • Retention, return incentives, and genuine collaboration keep gains shared
From critique to practice: decolonizing internationalization is not a rejection of global engagement — it is a demand that engagement be reciprocal, humble, and just. Concretely: audit whose knowledge counts, whose voices set the agenda, who bears the costs, and who captures the benefits — then redesign partnerships so the answer is genuinely shared. This is the ethical core of "internationalization of higher education for society" (de Wit & Jones).
Equity & ethics

Whose knowledge, whose access, whose benefit?

A growing body of scholarship asks internationalization to confront its inequities and colonial legacies — and to build partnerships that are genuinely reciprocal.

Access & inclusion

  • Mobility and global learning skew toward the advantaged
  • "Internationalization at home" democratizes the benefits
  • Funding, credit-bearing options, and advising broaden participation
  • Disability, first-generation, and low-income access remain gaps

Decolonizing internationalization

  • Flows and prestige favor the Global North and English
  • Curricula and rankings privilege Western knowledge
  • Scholars (e.g. de Wit & Jones, Stein, Leask) urge critical, inclusive models
  • Center local knowledge systems and epistemic diversity

Sustainable partnerships

  • Reject "helicopter" research that extracts without benefit
  • Equitable authorship, funding, and capacity-building
  • Reciprocity over one-way recruitment or revenue flows
  • Environmental cost of mobility (carbon) enters the calculus
The reframing: leading scholars call for a shift from internationalization measured by activity counts and revenue toward "internationalization of higher education for society" — intentional, inclusive, ethical, and oriented to public good rather than prestige and profit.

  • Credit vs. degree mobility — a term abroad for home credit vs. a full credential abroad.
  • COIL — Collaborative Online International Learning; a form of virtual exchange.
  • Bologna Process / EHEA — European harmonization of degree cycles & ECTS credits.
  • SEVIS — U.S. system tracking F/J visa holders; managed by the DSO.
  • Credential evaluation — assessing foreign qualifications for equivalency.
  • Branch campus — a physical presence abroad awarding the home credential.
  • Soft power — influence gained through educational & cultural attraction.
